# Facilitator Guide

## Your role

You present the situation, portray stakeholders, protect the pace, clarify consequences, and make the players’ choices matter.

You are not trying to defeat the team. You are testing whether its recommendation can survive contact with evidence, institutions, affected communities, and limited time.

## Preparation

Allow 30 minutes for the first session.

1. Read the quick-start rules.
2. Read the full Copperline scenario.
3. Copy or print one character per player.
4. Copy or print the public player handouts.
5. Keep the facilitator-only evidence and faction sections private.
6. Prepare four markers for the civic tracks.
7. Set a visible timer.

If fewer than six people play, leave the unused roles in the staff directory. Any player may consult an absent staff member once per investigation scene to obtain basic professional knowledge. This never grants a professional move or roll bonus.

## Opening script

Read or paraphrase:

> Welcome to Bellwether. You are the municipal team responsible for a recommendation on the Copperline proposal. There is no hidden perfect answer. Your job is to investigate, negotiate, and make the most defensible choice you can before the hearing.
>
> Anyone may pause or redirect material without explanation. Humor should target systems, professional habits, and fictional institutions—not residents or marginalized communities.
>
> We have two hours. The clock is real. So are the consequences.

Give players three minutes to read their characters. Ask each player to introduce:

- Name and role
- Most useful specialty
- One concern about the assignment

## Recommended pacing

| Clock     | Scene                   | Direction                                                 |
| --------- | ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| 0:00–0:10 | Welcome and rules       | Explain checks, specialties, support, moves, and tracks   |
| 0:10–0:20 | Staff briefing          | Read the public assignment and take questions             |
| 0:20–0:45 | Investigation           | Let every player lead one action                          |
| 0:45–1:10 | Stakeholder forum       | Run three focused conversations                           |
| 1:10–1:25 | Recommendation workshop | Require a development program and four to six commitments |
| 1:25–1:50 | Public hearing          | Present three challenges and obtain a decision            |
| 1:50–2:00 | Epilogue and debrief    | Describe consequences and gather immediate reactions      |

Give a five-minute warning before every scene transition.

## Adjudicating checks

Use DC 13 by default.

Use DC 10 when the team has strong evidence, an established relationship, adequate authority, or a well-designed approach.

Use DC 16 when the team lacks leverage, seeks an extraordinary concession, or acts after allowing a major risk to mature.

Before the roll, state:

- What success accomplishes
- What a likely cost would affect
- Whether a specialty applies

After the roll, ask the player to describe the professional action. Then describe the institutional or community response.

### Apply consequences consistently

Change a track only when the action affects that public condition. A spelling mistake should not reduce Equity. Ignoring an affected community might.

Do not reduce multiple tracks for one ordinary failed check. Reserve multiple effects for explicit recommendation tradeoffs and severe hearing consequences.

### Protect essential information

On a setback:

- Reveal the basic fact.
- Withhold the leverage, favorable interpretation, or stakeholder commitment.
- Apply a cost or complication.

Example: a poor environmental-records check still reveals that the wetland map is obsolete. A stronger result also reveals the grant program that can pay for restoration.

## Portraying stakeholders

Every faction has:

- A public position
- An underlying need
- A fear
- Leverage
- Conditions for support

Begin with the public position. Reveal the underlying need when players listen, ask useful questions, cite evidence, or use an appropriate professional move.

Do not portray disagreement as ignorance. Stakeholders may understand the facts and weigh them differently.

Reward specific offers. “We will address displacement” is not specific. “The town will seed a community land trust with $1.2 million before the first occupancy permit” is specific.

## Using the civic tracks

Keep all four tracks visible.

When a track changes:

1. Name the change.
2. State why it occurred.
3. Move the marker.

Example:

> Public Trust rises from 4 to 5 because the team disclosed the notice defect before the hearing and proposed a cure.

Allow players to challenge the reasoning. If they identify a genuine inconsistency, correct it.

Do not let players farm points by repeating the same action or promise.

## Recommendation design

Give the team the recommendation worksheet and commitment menu. Require:

- One development program
- Four to six commitments
- A responsible party for every commitment
- A funding or implementation source
- An acknowledged tradeoff
- An unresolved risk

The team may invent a commitment. Compare it to the listed examples and assign proportional effects. Tell players the effects before they finalize it.

If the group stalls, ask:

- What must be true for the applicant to stay?
- What must be true for Hearthside to share in the benefits?
- What cannot be rebuilt after it is lost?
- Which promise has no owner?
- What risk are you knowingly carrying into the hearing?

## Running the hearing

The hearing should feel like accumulated consequences, not random punishment.

Choose three challenges:

- One tied to evidence the team neglected
- One tied to a faction it did not satisfy
- One that tests how well the team explains its central tradeoff

Give each player an opportunity to lead one response. Accept evidence, agreements, and precise commitments established earlier.

If a professional move resolves a challenge, let it work. The characters are supposed to be competent.

## Describing the epilogue

First determine adoption from Political Support. Then determine the long-term category from all four tracks.

Describe:

- What is built or preserved
- What happens to Hearthside residents
- Whether the coalition survives
- Which implementation risk becomes real
- How the planners are remembered

Use the weakest track as the main complication and the strongest track as the main achievement.

## Debrief

Ask these questions before distributing the feedback form:

1. Which decision felt most consequential?
2. Which stakeholder changed your understanding of the proposal?
3. What did the track system capture well?
4. What did it oversimplify?
5. What would you change before another team plays?

## Optional fantasy texture

Bellwether may be played as an ordinary fictional municipality or a lightly magical one. If using fantasy:

- The map archive is a palimpsest atlas that reveals prior boundaries.
- Copper Run has a minor river spirit represented by the Riverkeepers.
- The abandoned rail once carried alchemical copper from mountain forges.
- The council chamber’s enchanted clock accelerates during repetitive testimony.
- The zoning code is physically bound in seven volumes and occasionally cross-references sections that do not yet exist.

Magic may reveal information or create atmosphere. It cannot erase displacement, manufacture public consent, or replace implementation.

## Facilitator restraint

Do not:

- Introduce a secret villain to simplify the conflict.
- Make one profession the required hero.
- Hide the basic scenario behind successful rolls.
- Allow a single persuasive speech to erase a faction’s material needs.
- Treat adoption as proof of good planning.
- extend a scene merely because every prepared detail has not appeared.

When uncertain, disclose the tradeoff and let the team choose.
